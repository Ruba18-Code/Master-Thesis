\section{Contact Angle Measurement}
\textbf{NEED IMPROVEMENTS}
The contact angle ($\theta_C$) is geometrically defined 
as the angle formed by a liquid at the three-phase boundary 
where the liquid, gas, and solid phases intersect. 
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:contact_angle_definition}, 
assuming an ideal, planar, and smooth surface, the equilibrium 
contact angle is governed by Young's equation:

\begin{equation}
    \gamma_{sg} = \gamma_{sl} + \gamma_{lg} \cos\theta
    \label{eq:young}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{sg}$, $\gamma_{sl}$, and $\gamma_{lg}$ represent 
the solid-gas, solid-liquid, and liquid-gas interfacial energies 
(or surface tensions), respectively. 
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Images/Contact_angle.png} 
    \caption{A diagram that shows the contact angle and interphase-energy between 
    3 phases (gas, liquid, solid). From \cite{wikicontact}.}
    \label{fig:contact_angle_definition}
\end{figure}

Since interfacial energy directly dictates the wettability of 
a substrate, a correlation exists between this parameter 
and the surface hydrophilicity; lower contact angles 
consistently signify higher hydrophilicity. 
Figure~\ref{fig:contact_angles} demonstrates the 
contact angle behavior across different surfaces, 
ranging from highly hydrophobic ($\theta > 90^\circ$) 
to highly hydrophilic ($\theta \to 0^\circ$).
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Images/Surface_tension.png} 
    \caption{Diagram of different contact angles. A corresponds to
    an hydrophobic surface. Surfaces B, C and S are sorted from less to
    more hydrophilic. From \cite{wikicontacts}.}
    \label{fig:contact_angles}
\end{figure}

Due to its experimental simplicity and non-destructive 
nature, quantifying this angle using water 
(Water Contact Angle, WCA) is a widely adopted method 
in literature to assess the state of glass surfaces 
\cite{CITA_METODO}. Surface hydrophilicity plays a 
critical role in the success of optical contact bonding, 
as a lower contact angle correlates with a higher density 
of active silanol (Si-OH) groups on the surface 
\cite{CITA_BONDING}. These hydroxyl groups are 
essential for initiating the intermolecular hydrogen 
networks required for a strong, spontaneous, and void-free union.
