\section{Optical Contact Bonding}
\label{sec:optical_contact_bonding}
Optical contact bonding is a glueless joining process in which two closely 
conformal surfaces adhere purely through intermolecular forces, without the 
need for adhesives or intermediate layers \cite{rammhandbook}. 
This property makes it particularly attractive for the hermetic sealing of Laser-Written Vapor Cells 
and other glass-based atomic sensors, where outgassing from adhesives, 
thermal incompatibility, or optical absorption at the bond interface can 
compromise device performance \cite{art2025}.

Intermolecular forces are, in isolation, insufficient to create a robust bond 
between two substrates. However, when mating surfaces conform to each other 
with nanometric precision, the resulting \textit{intimate contact} over a 
sufficiently large area produces intermolecular interactions with a measurable 
macroscopic effect \cite{haisma1994}. In practice, this imposes strict 
roughness and waviness requirements on both surfaces \cite{rammhandbook}. 
When these requirements are met and appropriate surface treatments are applied, 
bonding energies comparable to that of the bulk material 
(\SI{2.5}{\joule\per\meter\squared}) can be achieved \cite{kalk2014}.

Achieving such bond strengths requires a carefully sequenced preparation 
protocol. The following sections describe the key elements of this protocol: 
material selection and surface quality requirements, chemical treatment, plasma 
treatment, annealing and mechanical pressure assistance. Each step builds on the previous 
one; together, they determine the final bond quality and make optical contact 
bonding a viable low-temperature process for LWVC fabrication \cite{li2013}.
\subsection{Types of Glasses \& Quality Requirements}
The success of optical contact bonding critically depends on both the material 
properties of the glass substrates and their surface quality. Different glass 
types exhibit distinct chemical compositions, thermal characteristics, and 
mechanical behaviors that directly influence bonding performance. This section 
examines the most commonly employed glasses and establishes the surface quality 
requirements necessary for achieving robust, void-free bonds.
\paragraph{Fused Silica and Quartz}
Fused silica ($\mathrm{SiO_2}$, amorphous) and quartz ($\mathrm{SiO_2}$, 
crystalline) represent the gold standard for optical contact bonding. 
As characterized by Brückner \cite{brueckner1970}, these materials offer 
several key advantages:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{High purity and homogeneity:} Ultra-high purity grades 
    ($>$99.99\% $\mathrm{SiO_2}$) minimize contamination and ensure uniform 
    bonding behavior across the interface.
\item \textbf{Low thermal expansion coefficient:} With a CTE of approximately 
    \SI{0.5e-6}{\per\kelvin}, fused silica undergoes minimal dimensional 
    changes, thereby reducing thermal stress at the interface.
\item \textbf{Excellent optical transmission:} High transparency from UV to IR 
    wavelengths (\SI{200}{\nano\meter} to \SI{2500}{\nano\meter}) 
    \cite{malitson1965}.
\item \textbf{Chemical inertness:} Resistance to most acids and organic solvents 
    permits aggressive cleaning treatments without substrate degradation.
\item \textbf{High damage threshold:} Superior resistance to laser-induced damage 
    makes these materials suitable for high-power systems \cite{ye2016}.
\end{itemize}
The primary distinction between fused silica and quartz lies in their structural 
organization: fused silica is amorphous and isotropic, while quartz is 
crystalline and exhibits optical anisotropy \cite{brueckner1970}. For most 
bonding applications---and in particular for LWVCs---synthetic fused silica is 
preferred due to its isotropic properties and the exceptional surface quality 
achievable through polishing \cite{meissner2002}.
\paragraph{Borosilicate Glasses}
Borosilicate glasses, particularly Borofloat 33 
(81\% $\mathrm{SiO_2}$, 13\% $\mathrm{B_2O_3}$, 
4\% $\mathrm{Na_2O/K_2O}$, 2\% $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$), have gained widespread 
adoption in MEMS fabrication and vacuum cell manufacturing 
\cite{schott_borofloat, possl1999}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Moderate thermal expansion:} A CTE of approximately 
    \SI{3.25e-6}{\per\kelvin} \cite{okada1984} is compatible with silicon 
    (\SI{2.6e-6}{\per\kelvin}), making this glass suitable for glass-to-silicon 
    bonding \cite{gosele2006}.
\item \textbf{Good chemical resistance:} Adequate resistance to standard cleaning 
    treatments, though less robust than fused silica \cite{schott_borofloat}.
\item \textbf{Cost-effectiveness:} Significantly lower cost compared to fused 
    silica while maintaining sufficient optical quality for many applications.
\item \textbf{Commercial availability:} Readily available in wafer formats with 
    polished surfaces meeting bonding requirements.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Other Optical Glasses}
Several specialized glass families address niche requirements. 
Aluminosilicate glasses offer higher mechanical strength, improved thermal 
shock resistance \cite{yang2023}, and lower permeation rates for certain 
gases---such as helium---which is particularly relevant for LWVC hermeticity 
\cite{carle2023, dellis2016}. Ultra-low-expansion glasses such as Zerodur, 
with CTE values near zero, are indispensable in applications demanding extreme 
dimensional stability, including space telescopes and lithography systems 
\cite{jedamzik2014}.
\paragraph{Surface Quality Requirements}
Table~\ref{tab:surface_requirements} summarizes the surface quality 
parameters critical for successful optical contact bonding 
\cite{gosele2006, possl1999}. The three most important metrics are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Surface Roughness:} RMS roughness below \SI{0.5}{\nano\meter} 
    and peak-to-valley roughness below \SI{2}{\nano\meter} over the bonding 
    area \cite{gosele2006} are required to ensure intimate contact and allow 
    intermolecular forces to dominate. 
\item \textbf{Flatness and Waviness:} Total thickness variation (TTV) must 
    remain below \SI{1}{\micro\meter} for \SI{100}{\milli\meter} diameter 
    wafers. Surface flatness must be better than $\lambda$/10 
    (approximately \SI{60}{\nano\meter} at \SI{633}{\nano\meter}) for 
    precision optical applications. Long-spatial-wavelength deviations 
    (waviness) are particularly detrimental because, unlike short-scale 
    roughness, they cannot be accommodated by elastic surface deformation 
    during bonding \cite{kalk2014}.   
\item \textbf{Particulate Contamination:} Bonding must be performed under 
    cleanroom conditions (Class 100 or better, ISO 5). Even sub-micron 
    particles can nucleate localized voids that propagate across the 
    interface; surface particle counts must therefore be minimized, 
    with particular attention to particles larger than 
    \SI{0.1}{\micro\meter} \cite{du2022}.
\end{enumerate}
