\subsection{Optical Contact Bonding}
Optical contact bonding is a glueless process whereby two closely conformal 
surfaces are joined, being held purely by intermolecular forces \cite{rammhandbook}. 
\\\\
Generally, intermolecular forces are not strong enough to create
a robust bond between two substrates. However, if the surfaces to be bound
are conformal with nanometric precision, a sufficient surface area is in close 
enough contact ---also known as \textit{intimate contact}--- for the intermolecular
interactions to have an observable macroscopic effect \cite{haisma1994}.
\\\\
In practice, this means 
that, in order to bond two glass surfaces together (e.g. two disks to create a wafer),
they must meet very strict roughness and waviness requirements \cite{rammhandbook}. 
In literature, bonding energies 
equivalent to the bond density within the bulk material (\SI{2.5}{\joule\per\meter\squared})
have been achieved \cite{kalk2014}. 
To obtain such strong bonds,
special treatments are often applied to the substrates, in order to ensure
surfaces are very clean and ready to form intermolecular
bonds between them. To do so, many chemical, plasma, pressure and thermal
treatments have proven to be effective \cite{rammhandbook, meissner2002}.
\\\\
Since Optical Contact Bonding does not require any adhesive, and low-temperature
methods have been developed during the last decades \cite{li2013}, it is a promising bonding
procedure for glass-based atomic sensors \cite{art2025}. 

\subsubsection{Types of Glasses \& Quality Requirements}
The success of optical contact bonding critically depends on both the material 
properties of the glass substrates and their surface quality. Different glass 
types exhibit distinct chemical compositions, thermal characteristics, and 
mechanical behaviors that directly influence bonding performance. This section 
examines the most commonly employed glasses in optical contact bonding applications 
and establishes the stringent quality requirements necessary for achieving robust, 
void-free bonds.

\paragraph{Fused Silica and Quartz}
Fused silica ($\mathrm{SiO_2}$, amorphous) and quartz ($\mathrm{SiO_2}$, crystalline) 
represent the gold standard for optical contact bonding. According to the 
comprehensive characterization by Brückner \cite{brueckner1970}, these materials 
offer several key advantages:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{High purity and homogeneity:} Ultra-high purity grades 
    ($>$99.99\% $\mathrm{SiO_2}$) minimize contamination and ensure uniform bonding 
    behavior across the interface.
\item \textbf{Low thermal expansion coefficient:} With a CTE of approximately 
    \SI{0.5e-6}{\per\kelvin}, fused silica exhibits minimal dimensional changes, 
    thereby reducing thermal stress at the interface.
\item \textbf{Excellent optical transmission:} High transparency from UV to IR 
    wavelengths (\SI{200}{\nano\meter} to \SI{2500}{\nano\meter}) \cite{malitson1965}.
\item \textbf{Chemical inertness:} Resistance to most acids and organic solvents 
    allows for aggressive cleaning treatments without substrate degradation.
\item \textbf{High damage threshold:} Superior resistance to laser-induced damage 
    makes these materials suitable for high-power systems \cite{ye2016}.
\end{itemize}
The primary distinction between fused silica and quartz lies in their structural 
organization: fused silica is amorphous and isotropic, while quartz is crystalline 
and exhibits optical anisotropy \cite{brueckner1970}. For most bonding applications, synthetic fused 
silica is preferred due to its isotropic 
properties and exceptional surface quality achievable through polishing \cite{meissner2002}.

\paragraph{Borosilicate Glasses}
Borosilicate glasses, particularly Borofloat 33 (81\% $\mathrm{SiO_2}$, 13\% $\mathrm{B_2O_3}$, 
4\% $\mathrm{Na_2O/K_2O}$, 2\% $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$), have gained widespread 
adoption in MEMS fabrication and vacuum cell manufacturing \cite{schott_borofloat,possl1999}. These glasses offer:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Moderate thermal expansion:} CTE of approximately 
    \SI{3.25e-6}{\per\kelvin} \cite{okada1984}, which is compatible with silicon 
    (\SI{2.6e-6}{\per\kelvin}), making them suitable for glass-to-silicon bonding \cite{gosele2006}.
\item \textbf{Good chemical resistance:} Adequate resistance to chemical treatments, 
    though less robust than fused silica \cite{schott_borofloat}.
\item \textbf{Cost-effectiveness:} Significantly lower cost compared to fused silica 
    while maintaining sufficient optical quality for many applications.
\item \textbf{Commercial availability:} Readily available in wafer formats with 
    polished surfaces meeting bonding requirements.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Other Optical Glasses}
Several specialized glass families are employed in niche applications:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Aluminosilicate glasses:} Higher mechanical strength and improved 
    thermal shock resistance compared to standard borosilicates \cite{yang2023}. Lower leak rates
    for some gasses like Helium \cite{carle2023, dellis2016}.
\item \textbf{Low-expansion glasses:} Featuring CTE values near zero, such as Zerodur, are essential for 
    applications requiring extreme dimensional stability (e.g., space telescopes, 
    lithography systems) \cite{jedamzik2014}.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Surface Quality Requirements}
Achieving successful optical contact bonding demands exceptionally high surface 
quality across multiple metrics \cite{gosele2006,possl1999}. The following parameters are critical:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Surface Roughness:} Root mean square (RMS) roughness must typically 
    be below \SI{0.5}{\nano\meter}, with peak-to-valley roughness less than 
    \SI{2}{\nano\meter} over the bonding area \cite{gosele2006}. 
    This ensures sufficient intimate 
    contact for intermolecular forces to dominate. 
\item \textbf{Flatness and Waviness:} Total thickness variation (TTV) should be 
    maintained below \SI{1}{\micro\meter} for \SI{100}{\milli\meter} diameter 
    wafers. Surface flatness, typically specified as peak-to-valley deviation over 
    the clear aperture, must be better than $\lambda$/10 (approximately 
    \SI{60}{\nano\meter} at \SI{633}{\nano\meter}) for precision optical 
    applications. Longer spatial wavelength deviations (waviness) are particularly 
    detrimental as they cannot be accommodated by surface deformation during bonding \cite{kalk2014}.
\item \textbf{Particulate Contamination:} The bonding environment must maintain 
    cleanroom conditions (Class 100 or better, ISO 5), as even sub-micron particles 
    can create localized voids that propagate across the interface. Particle counts 
    on the surface should be minimized, with particular attention to particles 
    larger than \SI{0.1}{\micro\meter} \cite{du2022}.
\end{enumerate}
