\subsection{Thermal Treatment}
\label{sec:thermal}
When two sufficiently flat and clean glass surfaces are brought into contact, 
the bonding process initiates through the formation of hydrogen bonds between 
opposing silanol groups \cite{kissinger1990}. These bonds can subsequently be 
converted into permanent covalent siloxane bonds via the condensation reaction:
\begin{equation}
    \equiv\!\mathrm{Si}\text{-}\mathrm{OH} + 
    \mathrm{HO}\text{-}\mathrm{Si}\!\equiv 
    \;\xrightarrow{T\,>\,100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}}\; 
    \equiv\!\mathrm{Si}\text{-}\mathrm{O}\text{-}\mathrm{Si}\!\equiv 
    \;+\; \mathrm{H_2O}
    \label{eq:siloxane2}
\end{equation}
For this reaction to proceed irreversibly, water must be effectively removed from the 
interfacial network \cite{rammhandbook}. This is accomplished through \textit{annealing}, 
which involves baking the bonded assembly in a strictly controlled thermal environment 
governed by three primary parameters: the temperature ramp, the equilibrium temperature, 
and the total annealing time.

The \textit{temperature ramp} (heating and cooling rate) must be kept as low as possible, 
typically restricted to fractions of a degree per minute. Rapid temperature alterations 
induce severe transient thermal gradients and differential stresses within the glass 
substrates. These thermal stresses can compromise the mechanical integrity of the assembly, 
degrade optical performance via stress-induced birefringence, or cause catastrophic 
delamination and structural cracking due to thermal shock \cite{yoder2018}.

In contrast, the \textit{equilibrium temperature} directly controls the kinetics of water 
diffusivity at the interface. Elevated temperatures accelerate moisture evacuation and 
promote localized viscous flow, which fundamentally increases atomic-scale surface 
conformity \cite{rammhandbook}. Generally, the interfacial surface energy increases 
progressively with higher annealing temperatures, eventually reaching a plateau. However, 
in low-to-moderate temperature regimes, this plateau does not reflect the bulk fracture 
energy of the material; instead, it represents a state of equilibrium determined by the 
maximum density of silanol-to-siloxano conversions achievable under the restricted thermal 
budget and the remaining interfacial moisture. Achieving true bulk-like bonding energies 
without plasma would require high thermal budgets (typically above \SI{600}{\celsius}) 
that are strictly incompatible with precision devices incorporating thin-film optical 
coatings, pre-structured channels, or integrated electronics \cite{li2013}. In these 
specialized scenarios---including LWVCs, where laser-written microstructures must 
preserve their exact structural dimensions---the integration of plasma activation becomes 
indispensable. Plasma treatment raises the initial surface energy and subsurface water 
diffusivity sufficiently to maximize the covalent bond density at temperatures as low 
as \SI{100}{\celsius} \cite{cho2001}, a strategy known as \textit{Low-Temperature Direct 
Bonding}. The characteristic evolution of bonding energy within this optimized thermal 
framework is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:annealingtemp}.

Regarding the \textit{annealing time}, the bond energy increases monotonically following 
a predictable logarithmic trend, a behavior observed during both dedicated thermal baking 
and prolonged storage at room temperature \cite{cho2001}:
\begin{equation}
\gamma \propto \ln\!\left(\frac{t}{\mu}\right),
\end{equation}
where $t$ represents the elapsed annealing time and $\mu$ is a temperature-dependent 
characteristic time constant. Thermal annealing effectively reduces $\mu$ by several orders 
of magnitude, accelerating the convergence toward maximum bond strength, governed by the surface silanol density. 
Consequently, typical annealing durations required to stabilize high-performance optical 
components range from 2 to 24 hours \cite{cho2001, yu2023, li2013, possl1999, trinh2022}, 
as detailed in Figure~\ref{fig:annealing_time}.
