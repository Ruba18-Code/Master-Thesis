\subsection{Thermal Treatment}
\label{sec:thermal}

When two sufficiently flat and clean glass surfaces are brought into contact, 
the bonding process initiates through the formation of hydrogen bonds between 
opposing silanol groups \cite{kissinger1990}. These bonds can subsequently be 
converted into permanent covalent siloxane bonds via the condensation reaction:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Si\text{-}OH + HO\text{-}Si} \rightleftharpoons 
\mathrm{Si\text{-}O\text{-}Si + H_2O}.
\label{eq:siloxane}
\end{equation}
For this reaction to proceed irreversibly, water must be removed from the 
interface \cite{rammhandbook}. This is accomplished through \textit{annealing}: 
baking the bonded assembly in a controlled thermal environment. The three 
parameters governing the outcome are the temperature ramp, the equilibrium 
temperature, and the annealing time.
\\\\
The \textit{temperature ramp} (heating rate) must be kept as low as possible. 
Rapid temperature increases induce differential thermal stresses in the glass 
substrates that can compromise mechanical and optical performance, or cause 
fracture at the bond interface.
\\\\
The \textit{equilibrium temperature} controls water diffusivity at the 
interface: higher temperatures accelerate moisture removal and promote viscous 
flow, which increases surface conformity \cite{rammhandbook}. However, elevated 
temperatures are incompatible with precision devices that incorporate optical 
coatings, pre-structured channels, or integrated electronics \cite{li2013}. 
In such cases---including LWVCs, where laser-written microstructures must be 
preserved---plasma activation is essential, as it raises surface energy and 
water diffusivity sufficiently to achieve strong bonds at temperatures as low 
as \SI{100}{\celsius} \cite{cho2001}. This approach is referred to as 
\textit{Low-Temperature Direct Bonding}.
\\\\
Regarding \textit{annealing time}, bond energy increases monotonically 
following a logarithmic trend, whether through dedicated annealing or through 
prolonged room-temperature contact \cite{cho2001}:
\begin{equation}
\gamma \propto \ln\!\left(\frac{t}{\mu}\right),
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the annealing time and $\mu$ is a temperature-dependent time 
constant. Annealing effectively reduces $\mu$, allowing maximum bond energy 
to be reached orders of magnitude faster than at room temperature. Typical 
annealing durations for optical applications range from 2 to 24 hours 
\cite{cho2001, yu2023, li2013, possl1999, trinh2022}, as illustrated in 
Figure~\ref{fig:annealing_time}.
