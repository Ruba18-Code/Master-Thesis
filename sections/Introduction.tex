
\chapter{Introduction and Motivation}
\section{Historical Evolution of Sensing Technologies}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------
A sensor is an analytical device designed to detect and respond
to a specific input stimulus from the physical environment,
translating it into a measurable analog or digital signal. These
stimuli encompass a vast array of environmental phenomena,
including pressure, force, flow, light, heat, motion, and
moisture; the resulting output is typically converted into an
electrical signal that can be processed, measured, and
analyzed~\cite{javaid2021}.

Sensing instruments have been developed throughout human history,
with some of the earliest documented mechanisms dating back nearly
two millennia. Early devices relied entirely on mechanical,
hydraulic, and pneumatic feedback rather than electronic
transduction. Heron of Alexandria (62~CE), for instance,
designed an automatic mechanism for opening temple doors by
harnessing the thermal expansion of air, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:herondoor}. Zhang Heng's seismoscope
(132~CE) employed an inertia-based pendulum to detect and
indicate the direction of distant earthquakes, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:seismoscope}.

The precursors of modern quantitative sensors began to emerge
during the 17th century. Contemporary sensors generally share
three interconnected elements: (i)~a receptor that interacts with
the target analyte or stimulus; (ii)~a transducer that converts
that interaction into a characteristic signal; and (iii)~a
data-processing system that produces measurable output~\cite{pirzada2023}.
Galileo Galilei's thermoscope, which monitored temperature through
the thermal expansion of air, is an early example of this
framework; it was later improved by Santorio Santorio, who added a
numerical scale to create the first quantitative
thermometer~\cite{middleton1966}.

The Industrial Revolution (18th--19th centuries) demanded more
diverse and sophisticated monitoring tools, and it was during this
era that the concept of transduction ---translating a physical
phenomenon into an actionable, measurable output--- became firmly
established. Essential analytical tools such as the barometer,
litmus paper, the Davy safety lamp, and early mechanical
thermostats were developed to ensure industrial safety and process
control~\cite{pirzada2023,duncan1947}.

The 19th century marked a critical turning point, shifting
sensor output from purely mechanical displacements toward
electrical signals. Johann Schweigger's galvanometer (1820)
enabled precise measurement of micro-currents and paved the way
for electrical instrumentation. Pierre and Jacques Curie's
discovery of the piezoelectric effect in 1880 then enabled
sensors that convert mechanical stress directly into measurable
electrical charge~\cite{curie1880}.

The mid-20th century brought solid-state electronic sensors,
transforming global industries. The invention of the
point-contact transistor at Bell Labs in 1947~\cite{bardeen1948}
enabled smaller, more efficient electronics, and the subsequent
growth of the semiconductor industry made sensors progressively
more compact, efficient, and affordable. The introduction of
integrated circuits (ICs) in the late 1950s and 1960s
facilitated micro-miniaturisation and the integration of
multiple sensing functions onto a single silicon
substrate~\cite{kilby1976}. By the 1960s and 1970s these
solid-state sensors had become widespread across aerospace,
automotive, and telecommunications sectors, forming the
technological backbone of modern monitoring and control systems.
This relentless drive toward miniaturisation and integration set
the stage for an entirely new class of devices capable of
combining mechanical and electronic functionality at the
microscale.

\section{Microelectromechanical Systems (MEMS)}
Microelectromechanical Systems (MEMS) are devices with
characteristic lengths between \SI{1}{\micro\meter} and
\SI{1}{\milli\meter} (Figure~\ref{fig:scaleofthings}) that
combine electrical and mechanical components fabricated using
integrated-circuit batch-processing
technologies~\cite{memshandbook}. Silicon, the primary
structural and active material, is Earth-abundant,
cost-effective, and can be produced to extraordinary levels of
purity and crystalline perfection. Its processing relies on thin
deposited films that are intrinsically amenable to
miniaturisation, while photolithographic patterning enables
precise and reproducible definition of device geometries at
microscale dimensions. Most significantly from a commercial
standpoint, MEMS benefit from the same batch-fabrication
paradigm as microelectronic circuits: a single semiconductor
wafer simultaneously yields hundreds of identical devices
(Figure~\ref{fig:wafer_example}), dramatically reducing
per-unit costs and enabling large-scale
deployment~\cite{petersen1982}.

The exceptional purity and crystalline perfection achievable in
silicon substrates translate directly into optimised mechanical
properties~\cite{petersen1982}. As a consequence, MEMS devices
have attained levels of precision, sensitivity, and long-term
stability that were previously unachievable in macroscopic
mechanical systems, enabling their widespread adoption in consumer
electronics, biomedical instrumentation, environmental monitoring,
and precision metrology.

\section{Atomic Sensors}
\label{sec:atomic_sensors}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------

The demand for even more precise and stable measurements has
driven the development of quantum sensors based on atomic
spectroscopy. Atom-based quantum sensors exploit the highly
stable, reproducible, and sharply defined energy levels of atoms
to measure physical quantities~\cite{kitching2011,brown2025}. In
their basic form, these sensors prepare a specific quantum state
in a gas-phase atomic ensemble using laser light or
electromagnetic fields, and then measure a property of that state
to determine the value of the physical quantity of interest.

At their core, all atomic sensors share three main components:
a light source, a collection of identical atoms, and an optical
detection method, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:basicvaporcell}.
Modern designs use high-performance diode lasers, electro-optic
modulators, and sensitive photodetectors to approach fundamental
quantum limits.

\subsection{Vapor Cell Implementation}
\label{sec:vapor_cell}
One of the most common and compact implementations of an atomic sensor is
the vapor cell: a sealed glass container filled with an
alkali-metal gas~\cite{kitching2011}. A laser beam tuned to one
of the optical transitions of the atoms passes through the cell,
and a photodetector monitors the transmitted light or
fluorescence. This straightforward setup allows precise control
and readout of atomic quantum states while keeping the device
small and portable.

Depending on the specific sensing modality ---such as atomic clocks, 
spectroscopy, or magnetometry--- the laser light can manipulate 
different atomic properties, ranging from the excitation of 
populations across internal electronic energy levels to the transfer 
of angular momentum. In configurations that rely on the latter, 
this interaction drives a process known as \textit{optical pumping}, 
which polarizes the collective atomic spin states. In either case, 
the overall performance is typically tied to how effectively 
these laser-prepared quantum states can be preserved against 
environmental decoherence, which arises from both intrinsic 
spin-exchange collisions between alkali atoms and interactions with 
the physical boundaries of the container. 
Inside the cell, alkali atoms move rapidly due to thermal energy
and constantly collide with the walls, depolarising their spin
states. To extend the spin coherence time~$T_2$ beyond the
microsecond regime imposed by these collisions, two main
strategies are used~\cite{brown2025}.

The first strategy fills the cell with an inert buffer gas such
as nitrogen, helium, or neon. This gas slows the diffusion of
atoms toward the walls without destroying their spin state. For
the fundamental diffusion mode of a cylindrical cell, the
wall-collision contribution to the relaxation rate in the dark
is~\cite{lucivero2022}:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_\text{wd} = \left[ \left(\frac{\pi}{l}\right)^2
                       + \left(\frac{2.405}{r}\right)^2 \right]
                       \frac{D_0}{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{T}{273.15}},
    \label{eq:wall_relaxation}
\end{equation}
where $l$ is the cell interaction length, $r$ is the internal
radius, $D_0$ is the diffusion constant at standard conditions
(273~K, 760~Torr), and $\eta$ is the buffer gas number density
in amagat~\cite{lucivero2022}. Increasing~$\eta$ reduces
diffusion-driven losses but introduces a trade-off: collisions
between alkali atoms and gas molecules broaden the optical
transitions as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\nu_\text{pb} = \gamma_\text{pb}\,\eta,
    \label{eq:pressure_broadening}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_\text{pb}$ is the pressure-broadening coefficient
of the chosen gas~\cite{lucivero2022}. This broadening limits
optical pumping efficiency and spectral resolution, establishing
an optimal buffer gas pressure that balances coherence time and
spectral performance.

The second strategy coats the inner walls with materials such as
paraffin or alkene-based films. These coatings allow atoms to
bounce off the walls thousands of times without losing their spin
state, pushing~$T_2$ toward the second scale.

\subsection{Measurement Principle}
\label{sec:measurement_principle}
Fundamentally, atomic sensors rely on monitoring how the discrete quantum 
states of atoms ---such as their internal energy levels or spin 
orientations--- are perturbed by the environment~\cite{kitching2011}. 
When an atom interacts with an external field or physical quantity, this 
interaction either shifts its specific electronic or hyperfine energy 
levels, or lifts the degeneracy of its magnetic sublevels (Zeeman states). 
By precisely measuring these energy splits or the resulting spin dynamics, 
the properties of the perturbation can be accurately determined. 
The measurement cycle typically follows three steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{State preparation.}
          By utilizing light-matter interactions, the system is initialized 
          into a well-defined initial quantum state. This configuration 
          establishes the necessary baseline population distribution or collective 
          spin orientation required for the specific sensing task.
    \item \textbf{State evolution.}
          The prepared state evolves under the total Hamiltonian
          $\hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \hat{H}_\text{int}$, where
          $\hat{H}_\text{int}$ encodes the interaction with the
          field or quantity being measured~\cite{brown2025}. The quantum
          state accumulates a phase
          \begin{equation}
              \phi_\text{int} = \frac{1}{\hbar}
                  \int H_\text{int}\,\mathrm{d}t
                  = \frac{\mu F}{\hbar}\,T,
              \label{eq:phase}
          \end{equation}
          where $\mu$ is the coupling parameter, $F$ the field
          strength (or physical quantity), and $T$ the interaction time. For a
          magnetometer, $F = B$ and $\mu$ is the magnetic
          moment; for an inertial sensor, $F = \Omega$ and
          $\mu$ is the angular momentum.
    \item \textbf{Readout.}
          The final state is interrogated optically by measuring
          the transmission, fluorescence, or polarisation rotation
          of a probe beam, which encodes $\phi_\text{int}$
          and~$F$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Advantages of Atomic Sensors}
\label{sec:advantages}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

Atoms are ideal sensing elements because they satisfy four key
requirements: they are identical, isolatable, interfaceable, and
understandable~\cite{brown2025}. All atoms of a specific isotope
are perfectly identical, so a well-designed sensor is free of
manufacturing variations in its sensing element. Their energy
levels and responses to external fields are precisely known from
quantum electrodynamics, allowing systematic errors to be
rigorously controlled. Their strong interaction with resonant
photons makes them straightforward to manipulate and read out
using compact diode lasers and detectors. And because their
interactions with light and fields can be calculated from first
principles with high accuracy, realistic device simulations
require no empirical guesswork.

\subsection{Choice of Atomic Species}
\label{sec:species}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

The vast majority of practical atomic sensors use rubidium
($^{87}$Rb) and cesium ($^{133}$Cs)~\cite{brown2025}. Their
simple hydrogen-like electron structure is thoroughly documented,
providing exact data on transition frequencies and collision
cross-sections~\cite{foot2005}. Narrow-linewidth diode lasers are
readily available for the primary $D_1$ and $D_2$ transitions of
both elements, greatly simplifying the optical setup. Both species
produce a stable vapor at moderate temperatures between 80 and
130~$^\circ$C, enabling sealed glass cells without complex vacuum
pumps. Their ground-state hyperfine splittings ---\SI{9.192631770}{GHz}
for the $^{133}$Cs clock transition that defines the international
second, and \SI{6.834682610}{GHz} for $^{87}$Rb~\cite{ginges2017}--- lie
in a range directly accessible by standard microwave electronics.

\subsection{Quantum Noise and the Standard Quantum Limit}
\label{sec:sql}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

The fundamental noise floor of any atomic sensor arises from
quantum projection noise~\cite{kitching2011,brown2025}: each
measurement collapses an atom into a definite energy eigenstate
according to quantum probability, introducing unavoidable
shot-to-shot randomness. For $N$ independent atoms with
coherence time~$T_2$, the Standard Quantum Limit is
\begin{equation}
    \delta S \approx \frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\;
                     \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{N\,T_2}}
    \qquad\left[\frac{[\text{units of }S]}{\sqrt{\text{Hz}}}\right].
    \label{eq:sql}
\end{equation}
This expression captures the central design trade-off: larger atom
number~$N$ or longer coherence time~$T_2$ directly lowers the noise
floor. For short measurement times ($T \ll T_2$), the minimum
detectable field is
\begin{equation}
    \delta F_\text{min} = \frac{\hbar\,\delta\varphi}{\mu\,T},
\end{equation}
while for $T > T_2$ repeated measurements of duration~$T_2$ over
total averaging time~$\tau$ give
\begin{equation}
    \delta F_\text{min} = \frac{\hbar}{\mu\,\sqrt{N\,T_2\,\tau}}.
\end{equation}
In practice, technical noise ---laser intensity fluctuations,
frequency drift, imperfect magnetic shielding--- usually dominates
over quantum projection noise in real devices, and minimising these
sources remains the primary engineering challenge.

Among these, atomic clocks and optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) 
represent the most mature vapor-cell technologies~\cite{kitching2011}. 
While clocks track time by locking a local oscillator to invariant 
hyperfine transitions, OPMs utilize circularly polarised laser light 
to align atomic spins, reading out their Larmor precession in an external 
magnetic field. OPMs operating in the spin-exchange relaxation-free 
regime suppress the line-broadening caused by spin-exchange collisions. 
This allows them to overcome technical noise floors and approach their 
fundamental quantum projection noise limits, enabling experimental 
sensitivities below $1\,\text{fT}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ ---matching 
superconducting \gls{SQUIDs} without any cryogenic infrastructure. This regime 
is detailed further in Section~\ref{sec:opm}.

More recent modalities exploit different physical mechanisms to target 
other quantities. Rydberg-atom sensors, for instance, utilize electrons 
excited to very high principal quantum numbers, which creates giant electric 
dipole moments. A multi-laser excitation scheme establishes 
Electromagnetically Induced Transparency (EIT) between the ground state 
and the Rydberg level; any external electric field shifts the Rydberg 
states via the Stark effect, disrupts the coherence, and maps the field 
onto macroscopic changes in light transmission. Although their ultimate 
theoretical QPN limit lies in the pV~$\text{cm}^{-1}$ regime, practical 
experimental sensitivities of $10~\mu\text{V}\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ or better 
are currently achievable. Light-pulse atom interferometers (LPAIs), by 
contrast, offer the highest performance for inertial sensing through 
matter-wave interference, but require laser-cooled atoms and complex 
high-vacuum chambers, greatly increasing size, weight, and power (SWaP).

Despite these remarkable capabilities, vapor-cell-based sensors share a common 
bottleneck: the encapsulation cell itself. Existing fabrication 
technologies ---primarily silicon MEMS with anodic bonding, or 
photolithographic glass cells--- impose planar geometries, limit optical 
access, constrain the choice of sealing process, and are incompatible 
with organic anti-relaxation coatings. They also restrict integration 
with photonic components to hybrid, off-chip architectures. Addressing 
these limitations requires a fundamentally different manufacturing approach 
---one that combines the geometric freedom of femtosecond laser writing 
with the precision of glass microfabrication. This is the starting point 
for the technology that is the focus of this thesis, the Laser-Written Vapor Cell.

\section{Laser-Written Vapor Cells (LWVCs)}
\label{sec:lwvcs}

Laser-Written Vapor Cells (LWVCs) represent a novel generation of chip-scale atomic devices 
designed to overcome the aforementioned technological constraints by directly inscribing 
arbitrary three-dimensional cavities into commercial fused-silica substrates. Originally 
introduced by Lucivero et al.\ and patented by the Italian company \mbox{QSENSATO~Srl}~\cite{lucivero2022,qsensato_web}, 
LWVCs combine the 3D structuring freedom of femtosecond laser micromachining with the 
excellent optical quality and chemical inertness of fused silica.

\subsection{Fabrication Principle}
\label{sec:lwvc_fabrication}

The fabrication of LWVCs relies on two core techniques, each described in detail in the 
following chapter. First, the FLICE process (Femtosecond Laser Irradiation followed by 
Chemical Etching) inscribes arbitrary buried cavities ---optical sensing channels, 
micro-strainers, and reservoir chambers--- directly inside the monolithic substrate 
(Section~\ref{sec:flice}), as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:lwvc_anatomy}. Second, once a 
non-evaporable getter (NEG) dispenser pill is placed in the reservoir, the cell is 
hermetically sealed via optical contact bonding 
(Section~\ref{sec:optical_contact_bonding}). Post-sealing, a focused laser beam activates 
the NEG through the transparent substrate, releasing alkali-metal vapor ---typically 
$^{87}$Rb or $^{133}$Cs--- while simultaneously regulating the internal nitrogen (\ce{N2}) 
buffer gas pressure~\cite{lucivero2022}, with a demonstrated tuning resolution as fine as 
\SI{1}{Torr}~\cite{dyer2023}.

\subsection{Key Advantages over Conventional MEMS Cells}
\label{sec:lwvc_advantages}

This fabrication strategy yields four structural and operational benefits that are 
unattainable with conventional photolithographic MEMS vapor cells.

\textbf{True 3D monolithic integration.} The FLICE technique inscribes fully buried, 
arbitrarily shaped cavities in three dimensions within a single substrate, breaking free 
from the planarity constraints of photolithographic MEMS. This geometric freedom 
enables a minimal stand-off distance: sensing channels can be positioned less than 
\SI{1}{\milli\meter} ---and potentially just a few tens of micrometers--- below the 
outer surface~\cite{lucivero2022,zanoni2024}, which is vital for detecting rapidly 
decaying fields from micro-scale targets such as single neurons or individual magnetic 
nanoparticles.

\textbf{Compatibility with anti-relaxation coatings.} Because optical contact bonding 
operates near room temperature ---unlike the elevated temperatures required by anodic 
bonding--- the inner walls of LWVCs are fully compatible with organic anti-relaxation 
coatings such as paraffin or alkene-based films. These coatings push the spin coherence 
time toward the second scale~\cite{lucivero2022}, a performance regime entirely 
inaccessible to standard MEMS cells.

\textbf{Passive buffer gas control.} The integrated NEG dispensers enable post-sealing 
tuning of the internal \ce{N2} pressure without any external vacuum infrastructure, 
eliminating a key fabrication bottleneck of conventional MEMS cells~\cite{lucivero2022}.

\textbf{Wafer-level scalability.} Because femtosecond laser writing is a 
software-controlled, maskless direct-write process, all fabrication steps ---FLICE, 
HF etching, getter insertion, bonding, and laser activation--- are compatible with 
wafer-scale batch processing~\cite{corrielli2021}, enabling tens to hundreds of identical 
devices per substrate, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:lwvc_wafer}.

\subsection{Photonic Circuit Integration}
\label{sec:lwvc_pic}

A further consequence of the monolithic 3D architecture is its intrinsic compatibility 
with Photonic Integrated Circuits (PICs), establishing a path toward chip-scale atomic 
clocks, magnetometers, and quantum memories with significantly reduced footprints. Two 
integration routes are possible. In the hybrid approach, a dedicated waveguide coupler 
connects the sensing volume to an external photonic waveguide or fiber network, as 
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:atomic_pic}. In the fully monolithic approach, the same 
femtosecond laser locally modifies the refractive index of the surrounding bulk glass to 
write optical waveguides directly adjacent to the atomic channels 
(Figure~\ref{fig:lw_waveguide}), routing probe and pump beams to the vapor volume 
on-chip and eliminating mode-matching losses and alignment tolerances entirely.

\subsection{State of the Art and Performance}
\label{sec:lwvc_state}

The rapid maturation of the LWVC platform is best appreciated by comparing its consecutive 
published generations. The first devices featured \mbox{$1\,\text{mm} \times 1\,\text{mm}$} 
sensing channels (9.5~mm long) and demonstrated proof-of-principle sub-Doppler saturated 
absorption spectroscopy and single-beam optical magnetometry with rubidium 
vapor~\cite{lucivero2022}. The second generation scaled the channels down to 
$500~\si{\micro\meter} \times 500~\si{\micro\meter}$ in cross-section, added 
$0.75~\text{amg}$ of \ce{N2} buffer gas to simultaneously suppress wall-collision 
relaxation and eliminate radiation trapping, and demonstrated zero-field resonance 
(ZFR) magnetometry (Section~\ref{sec:zfrexp}) in a sensing volume of only 
$2.25~\text{mm}^3$~\cite{zanoni2024}. The resulting magnetic sensitivity of 
$\approx 1\,\text{pT}\,\text{Hz}^{-1/2}$ at 10~Hz places LWVCs at the forefront of 
compact, high-resolution magnetometry, proving competitive with significantly larger 
vapor-cell platforms.

This performance remains far from the fundamental limit. Atomic-shot-noise calculations 
project a sensitivity floor of $\approx 18\,\text{fT}\,\text{Hz}^{-1/2}$ at the optimal 
buffer gas density of 7.1~amg~\cite{zanoni2024}, indicating more than two orders of 
magnitude of improvement remain accessible as the technology matures. Regarding operating 
temperature, the present devices run at 90--100~$^\circ$C to sustain sufficient $^{87}$Rb 
vapor pressure; for applications involving biological samples, this thermal load can be 
mitigated by substituting $^{87}$Rb with $^{133}$Cs or metastable helium, both of which 
operate at lower temperatures~\cite{zanoni2024}.

Beyond precision magnetometry, the monolithic LWVC geometry opens new sensing paradigms 
that are explored in Section~\ref{chap:applications}.

\textbf{Scope of this thesis.} This work covers the fabrication, testing, and practical 
applications of LWVCs at QSENSATO~Srl. Starting from a thorough review of the 
underlying fabrication physics, it investigates the FLICE fabrication pipeline 
(Section~\ref{sec:flice}). After that, the optical contact bonding sealing process 
(Sections \ref{sec:optical_contact_bonding} and \ref{sec:optexp}) is analyzed. It then
