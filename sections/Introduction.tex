\chapter{Introduction and Motivation}
In order to better understand physical reality, a wide variety
of devices capable of measuring physical phenomena --such as temperature,
speed, pressure, time-- have been developed over the history of humanking.
These devices are known as sensors. Sensor is an analytical tool that recognize and responds to physical stimuli
including light, heat, and motion, moisture, pressure, or any other
environmental change. Sensors convert these stimuli into signals that can
be measured and analyzed.

However, the first modern sensors were developed in the 17th century (thermometer,
barometer and hydrometer \cite{Nagabooshanam et al.}.).
The earliest sensors were simple devices that responded to environmental changes.
For instance, the use of thermometers dates back to the 16th century,
with Galileo's invention of the thermoscope, which measured tempera-
ture changes through the expansion of air. 

During the industrial revolution (18th-19th century), the need to build and understand 
the behaviour of machines and the scientific revolution led to more
diverse and sophisticated sensors. In this time, the concept of translating
a physical phenomena intro measurable signals was introduced.
The 19th century marked a significant turning point in sensor technology.
The invention of the galvanometer by Johann Schweigger in 1820
allowed for the measurement of electric current, paving the way for
electrical sensors. In 1835, the first piezoelectric effect was discovered by
Pierre and Jacques Curie, leading to the development of piezoelectric
sensors that convert mechanical stress into electrical signals.

In the 20th century, the first electronic sensors were introduced. This transformed
industries by providing more accurate, reliable, and faster measurements. 
After WWII, sensors became progressively more compact, efficient and affordable 
thanks to the semicondcutor industry. By the 1960s and 1970s, sensors were being widely
used in aerospace, automotive, and telecommunications industries, often
forming the backbone of complex monitoring and control systems.
