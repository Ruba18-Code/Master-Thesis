\chapter{Introduction and Motivation}
\section{Historical Evolution}
A sensor is an analytical device designed to detect and respond to a specific input stimulus from 
the physical environment, translating it into a measurable analog or digital signal. These input 
stimuli encompass a vast array of environmental phenomena, such as pressure, force, flow, light, 
heat, motion, or moisture. The resulting output is typically converted into an electrical signal 
that can be subsequently processed, measured, and analyzed \cite{javaid2021}.

In order to better understand physical reality, a wide variety of sensors have been developed 
throughout human history, with some of the earliest documented mechanisms dating back nearly 
two millennia. Early sensing principles relied entirely on mechanical, hydraulic, and pneumatic 
feedback loops rather than electronic transduction. For instance, Heron of Alexandria developed 
an automatic mechanism for opening and closing temple doors in 62~CE. This device utilized the 
thermal expansion of air to trigger mechanical motion, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:herondoor}. 
Similarly, Zhang Heng's seismoscope (132~CE) employed an inertia-based internal pendulum to detect 
and indicate the direction of distant earthquakes, as detailed in Figure~\ref{fig:seismoscope}.

The precursor to modern quantitative sensors began to emerge during the 17th century. 
In contemporary engineering, these devices commonly rely on three key interconnected 
elements: (i) a receptor that specifically interacts with the target analyte or physical 
stimulus; (ii) a transducer that responds to this interaction by producing a characteristic 
signal; and (iii) a data-processing system that computes the signal into measurable 
quantities and generates usable data \cite{pirzada2023}. As one of the earliest examples of this 
evolving conceptual framework, Galileo Galilei invented the thermoscope, which monitored 
temperature variations through the thermal expansion of air. This qualitative device was 
subsequently improved by Santorio Santorio, who introduced a numerical scale, effectively 
transforming the instrument into the first measurable thermometer \cite{middleton1966}.

During the Industrial Revolution (18th to 19th century), the scientific imperative to analyze 
and optimize machine behavior demanded more diverse and sophisticated monitoring tools. 
It was during this era that the foundational concept of transduction---translating a 
physical phenomenon into an actionable, measurable output---was firmly established. 
Concurrently, essential analytical tools such as the barometer, litmus paper, the Davy safety 
lamp, and early mechanical thermostats were developed to ensure industrial safety and process 
control \cite{pirzada2023, duncan1947}.

The 19th century marked a critical turning point in sensor technology by shifting the output 
medium from purely mechanical displacements toward electrical signals. The invention of the 
galvanometer by Johann Schweigger in 1820 allowed for the precise measurement of micro-currents, 
effectively paving the way for electrical instrumentation. Later, in 1880, Pierre and Jacques 
Curie discovered the piezoelectric effect, a breakthrough that enabled the development of 
piezoelectric sensors capable of converting mechanical stress directly into measurable 
electrical charge \cite{curie1880}.

The mid-20th century witnessed the introduction of solid-state electronic sensors, 
transforming global industries by providing faster, more reliable, and highly accurate 
measurements. This era was revolutionized by the invention of the point-contact transistor 
at Bell Labs in 1947, which enabled the development of significantly smaller and more 
efficient electronic components \cite{bardeen1948}. Following World War II, the rapid 
growth of the semiconductor industry allowed sensors to become progressively more compact, 
efficient, and affordable. This technological trajectory was further propelled by the 
introduction of integrated circuits (ICs) in the late 1950s and 1960s, a breakthrough 
that facilitated the micro-miniaturization of devices and enabled the integration of 
multiple sensing functions onto a single silicon substrate \cite{kilby1976}. By the 
1960s and 1970s, these advanced solid-state sensors became widespread across the aerospace, 
automotive, and telecommunications sectors, ultimately forming the technological backbone of 
complex, modern monitoring and control systems. This relentless drive toward miniaturization and 
integration set the stage for an entirely new class of devices capable of combining mechanical and 
electronic functionality at the microscale.

\section{Microelectromechanical Systems (MEMS)}

Microelectromechanical Systems (MEMS) refer to devices with characteristic lengths between 
\SI{1}{\micro\meter} and \SI{1}{\milli\meter} (see Figure~\ref{fig:scaleofthings}) that combine 
electrical and mechanical components fabricated using integrated circuit batch-processing 
technologies \cite{memshandbook}. In recent decades, MEMS have found widespread application 
across a variety of fields, driven by several inherent advantages of the technology. 

Silicon, the primary structural and active material, is Earth-abundant, cost-effective, and can 
be produced to extraordinary levels of purity and crystalline perfection. Its processing relies 
on thin deposited films that are intrinsically amenable to miniaturization, while 
photolithographic patterning techniques enable highly precise and reproducible definitions 
of device geometries at microscale dimensions. Perhaps most significantly from a commercial 
standpoint, MEMS devices benefit from the same batch-fabrication paradigm as microelectronic 
circuits: a single semiconductor wafer simultaneously yields hundreds of identical devices, 
as exemplified in Figure~\ref{fig:wafer_example}, dramatically reducing per-unit costs and 
enabling large-scale deployment \cite{petersen1982}.

The exceptional purity and crystalline perfection achievable in silicon substrates translate directly 
into optimized mechanical properties, much as these same characteristics have long been exploited to 
maximize electronic performance, reliability, and reproducibility in integrated circuits \cite{petersen1982}. 
As a consequence, MEMS devices have attained levels of precision, sensitivity, and long-term stability that 
were previously unachievable in macroscopic mechanical systems, enabling their widespread adoption in consumer 
electronics, biomedical instrumentation, environmental monitoring, and precision metrology.

\section{Atomic Sensors}
\label{sec:atomic_sensors}

The demand for precise and stable measurements has driven the 
development of quantum sensors based on atomic spectroscopy. 
Atom-based quantum sensors exploit the highly stable, 
reproducible, and sensitive energy levels of atoms to measure 
physical quantities \cite{kitching2011,brown2025}. In their 
basic form, these sensors create a specific quantum state in 
a gas-phase atom using laser light or electromagnetic fields. 
They then measure a property of that state to determine the 
value of the physical quantity being tested.

At their core, all atomic sensors share three main components: 
a light source, a collection of identical atoms, and an 
optical method for detection, as shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:basicvaporcell}. Modern designs use 
high-performance diode lasers, electro-optic modulators, and 
sensitive photodetectors to reach fundamental quantum limits.

\subsection{Vapor Cell Implementation}
\label{sec:vapor_cell}

The most common and compact setup for an atomic sensor is the 
vapor cell. This is a sealed glass container filled with an 
alkali-metal gas \cite{kitching2011}. A laser beam tuned to 
one of the optical transitions of the atoms is shone through 
the cell, and a photodetector monitors the transmitted light 
or fluorescence. This simple setup allows researchers to 
control and read the quantum properties of atoms with high 
accuracy while keeping the device small.

Inside the cell, the alkali atoms move fast due to thermal 
energy and constantly collide with the walls. To keep the 
quantum spin state alive longer (extending the coherence time 
$T_2$ beyond the microsecond limit caused by these 
collisions), two main methods are used \cite{brown2025}. 

The first method fills the cell with an inert buffer gas 
mixture, such as nitrogen, helium, or neon. This gas slows 
down the movement of the atoms toward the walls without 
destroying their spin state. Under this regime, the 
contribution of wall collisions to the spin relaxation rate 
in the dark ($\Gamma_\text{wd}$) for the fundamental 
diffusion mode of a cylindrical cell structure is governed by 
the cell geometry and gas dynamics \cite{lucivero2022}:
\begin{equation}
  \Gamma_\text{wd} = \left[ \left(\frac{\pi}{l}\right)^2 
                     + \left(\frac{2.405}{r}\right)^2 \right] 
                     \frac{D_0}{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{T}{273.15}},
  \label{eq:wall_relaxation}
\end{equation}
where $l$ represents the cell interaction length, $r$ is the 
internal radius, $D_0$ is the specific diffusion constant at 
standard reference conditions (273~K and 760~Torr), and 
$\eta$ denotes the buffer gas number density in units of 
amagat \cite{lucivero2022}. 

While increasing the buffer gas density $\eta$ reduces 
diffusion-driven wall losses, it introduces an important 
physical trade-off: colisional interactions between the 
alkali atoms and the gas molecules cause pressure 
broadening ($\Delta\nu_\text{pb}$) of the optical transitions. 
This broadening scales linearly with the gas density:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\nu_\text{pb} = \gamma_\text{pb} \, \eta,
  \label{eq:pressure_broadening}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_\text{pb} $ is the specific pressure broadening 
coefficient of the chosen gas mixture \cite{lucivero2022}. 
This broadening blends the hyperfine states and limits the 
optical pumping efficiency, establishing an optimal gas 
pressure that balances spin coherence time and spectral 
resolution.

The second method coats the inner walls of the cell with 
special materials like paraffin or alkene-based substances. 
These coatings allow the atoms to bounce off the walls 
thousands of times without losing their spin state, effectively 
minimizing $\Gamma_\text{wd}$ and pushing $T_2$ close to 
one second.

The choice between these methods depends on the application, 
with vapor cells offering the best balance of size, weight, 
and power (SWaP) for magnetometers and basic atomic clocks.

\subsection{Measurement Principle}
\label{sec:measurement_principle}

All atomic sensors are fundamentally based on measuring the 
energy difference between discrete atomic states \cite{kitching2011}. 
When an atom interacts with an external field, its energy levels 
shift. This shift can be precisely measured to find the exact 
strength of the field. The measurement cycle follows three main 
steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{State preparation.}
        The atoms are put into a well-defined quantum state 
        using optical pumping. In this process, circularly 
        polarized laser light transfers angular momentum to 
        the atoms, lining up their spins along the laser 
        beam axis. This establishes the initial baseline vector 
        on the Bloch sphere before the system is exposed to 
        external perturbations.

  \item \textbf{State evolution.}
        The prepared state changes over time under the total 
        energy of the system, described by the Hamiltonian 
        $\hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \hat{H_\text{int}}$. Here, 
        $\hat{H_\text{int}}$ represents the interaction with 
        the external field being measured \cite{brown2025}. 
        The quantum state builds up a phase shift:
        \begin{equation}
          \phi_\text{int} = \frac{1}{\hbar}\int H_\text{int}\,\mathrm{d}t 
                          = \frac{\mu F}{\hbar}\,T,
          \label{eq:phase}
        \end{equation}
        where $\mu$ is the coupling parameter, $F$ is the 
        field strength, and $T$ is the interaction time. 
        
        As cited in the literature, for an atomic magnetometer, 
        $F$ corresponds to the external magnetic field $B$ 
        and $\mu$ is the magnetic moment of the atom. 
        Conversely, in the case of inertial sensors, $F$ 
        represents the physical rotation rate $\Omega$ and $\mu$ 
        is the angular momentum of the atom.

  \item \textbf{Readout.}
        The final state is checked optically by measuring the 
        transmission, fluorescence, or polarization change 
        of a probe laser beam, which reveals $\phi_\text{int}$ 
        and $F$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Advantages of Atomic Sensors}
\label{sec:advantages}

Atoms are ideal for sensing because they meet four key 
requirements: they are identical, isolatable, interfaceable, 
and understandable \cite{brown2025}. First, all atoms of a 
specific isotope are perfectly identical. This means a 
well-designed sensor does not suffer from manufacturing 
variations in its sensing element. 

Second, atomic energy levels and their reactions to external 
fields are very well known, allowing engineers to control 
and minimize systematic errors. For example, the core clock 
transition of cesium is independent of magnetic fields to 
first order, and warm gases in glass cells are largely 
unaffected by energy shifts from wall collisions. 

Third, atoms are easy to interface with, as their strong 
interaction with resonant photons allows users to control and 
read their states using compact diode lasers and detectors. 

Finally, atomic interactions with light and fields are 
understandable because they can be calculated from first 
principles with high accuracy, allowing realistic simulations 
without relying on guesswork.

\subsection{Choice of Atomic Species}
\label{sec:species}

The vast majority of practical atomic sensors use heavy alkali 
atoms, specifically $^{87}\text{Rb}$ and $^{133}\text{Cs}$ \cite{brown2025}. 
Their simple, 
hydrogen-like electron structure is well documented, providing 
exact data on transition frequencies and collision behaviors 
\cite{foot2005}. Narrow-linewidth diode lasers are readily 
available for the primary optical $D_1$ and $D_2$ transitions 
of both elements, which greatly simplifies the optical setup.

Both elements create a stable vapor at low pressure and 
moderate temperatures between 80 and 130~$^\circ\text{C}$, 
allowing the use of sealed glass cells without complex vacuum 
pumps. Additionally, their ground-state hyperfine splittings 
can be directly driven and measured using standard, high-performance 
microwave electronics \cite{brown2025}. Specifically, 
the clock transition of $^{133}\text{Cs}$ at 
$9.192631770~\text{GHz}$ defines the international second, while 
$^{87}\text{Rb}$ exhibits a ground-state splitting of 
$6.834682610~\text{GHz}$ \cite{ginges2017}. 

In magnetometry applications, the linear Zeeman shift and the 
resulting Larmor precession are determined by the precise, 
dimensionless ground-state hyperfine Landé $g_F$-factors. 
These fundamental constants dictate the predictable scaling between 
the measured atomic frequency shifts and the external magnetic 
field strength.
\subsection{Quantum Noise and the Standard Quantum Limit}
\label{sec:sql}

The fundamental noise floor of any atomic sensor comes from 
quantum projection noise \cite{kitching2011,brown2025}. Each 
measurement forces an atom into a specific energy state based 
on quantum probabilities, introducing unavoidable randomness 
between measurements. For $N$ independent atoms with a 
coherence time $T_2$, the noise floor follows the Standard 
Quantum Limit:
\begin{equation}
  \delta S \;\approx\; \frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\;
                        \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{N\,T_2}}
  \qquad \left[\frac{[\text{units of }S]}{\sqrt{\text{Hz}}}\right],
  \label{eq:sql}
\end{equation}
where $\partial S/\partial E$ shows how much the measured 
signal $S$ changes with an energy shift. This equation 
highlights the main design trade-off: using more atoms ($N$) 
or achieving a longer coherence time ($T_2$) lowers the 
noise floor.

For short measurement times ($T \ll T_2$), the smallest 
detectable field depends on the phase resolution of the 
readout system and the coupling strength $\mu$ between the 
field and the atom:
\begin{equation}
  \delta F_\text{min} = \frac{\hbar\,\delta\varphi}{\mu\,T}.
\end{equation}
When the measurement runs longer than the coherence time 
($T > T_2$), the sensitivity is calculated by adding up 
multiple independent measurements of length $T_2$. This yields 
a minimum detectable field that improves with the total 
averaging time $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
  \delta F_\text{min} = \frac{\hbar}{\mu\,\sqrt{N\,T_2\,\tau}}.
\end{equation}
In practice, technical noise sources—like laser intensity 
shifts, frequency drift, or imperfect magnetic shielding—usually 
dominate over quantum noise in real-world devices. Minimizing 
these everyday noise sources remains the primary engineering 
task.

Among these technologies, optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) 
are the most mature vapor-cell sensors \cite{kitching2011}. 
These devices track how atomic spins rotate in an external 
magnetic field after being lined up. Circularly polarized laser 
light aligns the atomic spins, and their natural rotation 
(Larmor precession) is driven and measured using optical 
techniques. OPMs operating in the spin-exchange relaxation-free (SERF) 
regime stop the line-broadening usually caused by collisions 
between atoms, allowing them to reach incredible sensitivities 
below $1\,\text{fT}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$. This matches the 
performance of superconducting SQUID magnetometers but completely 
avoids the need for expensive cryogenic cooling. 
This technique is explained further in section~\ref{sec:opm}.

Electric fields can also be measured with very high sensitivity 
in atomic gases by using Rydberg atoms. Rydberg states occur 
when an electron is excited to a very high principal quantum number, 
yielding a giant electric dipole moment that renders the atom highly 
sensitive to energy shifts via the Stark effect. To interrogate these 
systems, a multi-laser excitation scheme is employed to establish a coherent 
superposition of quantum pathways between the ground state and the 
Rydberg levels \cite{brown2025}. This configuration gives rise to 
Electromagnetically Induced Transparency (EIT), where destructive quantum 
interference prevents probe photon absorption, rendering the otherwise 
opaque atomic vapor transparent. Any external electric field perturbation 
shifts the Rydberg states, disrupting this coherence and mapping the field 
variations directly onto macroscopic changes in light transmission through 
the room-temperature gas cell. By measuring these shifts with microwave 
or laser spectroscopic tools, electric field sensitivities on the order 
of $10~\mu\text{V}\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ or better are achieved.

In contrast to small vapor cells, light-pulse atom 
interferometers offer the highest performance for measuring 
acceleration and rotation. However, they require laser-cooled 
atoms and complex high-vacuum chambers, which significantly 
increases their size, weight, and power demands. 

Today, field work in magnetometry and electrometry relies 
heavily on compact vapor cells, while the highest-performing 
gravimeters, gyroscopes, and clocks use ultra-cold atoms. 
Laser-written vapor cells, the focus of this thesis, offer a clear 
path to building miniature, mass-producible sensors that keep the 
core performance advantages of quantum technology.
\section{Laser-Written Vapor Cells (LWVCs)}
\label{sec:lwvcs}

Laser-Written Vapor Cells (LWVCs) represent a highly disruptive technology for making chip-scale 
atomic devices used in precision spectroscopy and integrated quantum sensors. Originally described 
by Lucivero et al. \cite{lucivero2022,qsensato_web}, one of the founders of the Italian company 
QSENSATO Srl, this technology is patented by the company and overcomes the design and manufacturing 
limits of traditional silicon microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) vapor cells. Specifically, 
LWVCs allow the maskless fabrication of arbitrary three-dimensional (3D) internal volumes directly 
within commercial glass substrates.

\subsection{Fabrication Principle}
\label{sec:lwvc_fabrication}
The operating principle and fabrication of these devices are based on the Femtosecond Laser Irradiation 
followed by Chemical Etching (FLICE) technique (detailed in Section~\ref{sec:flice}), implemented on a 
monolithic fused-silica host \cite{zanoni2024}. The process takes place in two sequential steps. 
First, a tightly focused femtosecond laser beam locally alters the molecular structure of the glass 
at the microscopic scale. This step makes the irradiated areas more susceptible to chemical etching 
by forming self-oriented nanogratings. Afterward, the substrate is immersed in a hydrofluoric acid (HF) 
solution, which selectively removes the modified material to carve out optical sensing channels, 
micro-strainer conduits, and reservoir chambers with micrometer-scale tolerances \cite{zanoni2024}.

The confinement and precise dosing of the active alkali metal, typically rubidium ($^{87}$\text{Rb}) 
or cesium ($^{133}$\text{Cs}), are achieved by placing non-evaporable getters (NEGs) into the cell reservoir. 
The reservoir is then sealed using optical contact bonding (see Section~\ref{sec:optical_contact_bonding} for 
a complete overview of the bonding process). The NEG material serves a dual purpose: when activated locally 
by a laser after sealing, it releases pure atomic vapor and simultaneously acts as a passive getter pump 
to control the nitrogen (\ce{N2}) buffer gas pressure. This completely eliminates the need for bulky external 
vacuum systems. A recent study demonstrated pressure tuning with a resolution as low as \SI{1}{Torr} 
using cesium dispensers \cite{dyer2023}, showing the versatility of this filling and activation process 
across different target pressures.

\subsection{Key Advantages over Competing Technologies}
\label{sec:lwvc_advantages}

The LWVC architecture offers a set of critical advantages for commercial quantum sensors that no single 
competing technology can simultaneously provide.

\textbf{Monolithic 3D Integration.} Unlike conventional semiconductor fabrication, which is strictly planar, 
FLICE inscribes fully buried, arbitrarily shaped cavities in three dimensions within the same monolithic 
substrate. This allows the direct co-integration of atomic sensing channels alongside laser-written optical 
waveguides and photonic integrated circuits (PICs), eliminating the performance losses and alignment issues 
associated with hybrid bonding methods.

\textbf{Minimal Stand-off Distance.} The sensing micro-channels can be positioned less than \SI{1}{\milli\meter} 
(and potentially just a few tens of micrometers) below the outer surface of the fused silica host. 
This provides a clear structural advantage: because magnetic or electric fields from small sources 
drop rapidly with distance, minimizing this separation is crucial for detecting signals from micro-scale 
targets like single neurons, microfluidic NMR samples, or magnetic nanoparticles \cite{zanoni2024}.

\textbf{Compatibility with Anti-Relaxation Coatings.} Because optical contact bonding does not require 
the high temperatures typical of anodic bonding in MEMS systems, the inner walls of LWVCs are fully 
compatible with organic anti-relaxation coatings, such as paraffin or alkene-based films. These coatings 
allow alkali atoms to undergo thousands of wall collisions without depolarizing, which significantly 
increases the transverse spin relaxation time.

\textbf{Precise Buffer Gas Control Without External Equipment.} The use of NEG dispensers enables 
post-sealing tuning of the internal \ce{N2} pressure without any external vacuum infrastructure. 
By controlling the activation time or by pre-loading a specific amount of an ungetterable noble gas, 
a wide range of target pressures can be achieved passively. This reduces system complexity for 
field-deployable sensors.

\textbf{Broad Optical Transparency and Material Inertness.} Fused silica is transparent across a 
wide spectral range, from deep UV to mid-infrared, and is chemically inert to alkali-metal vapors. 
This avoids the diffusion and wall-absorption issues that affect borosilicate glass and silicon 
substrates, protecting both cell lifetime and spectral accuracy.

\textbf{Wafer-Level Fabrication and Scalability.} A key advantage of the FLICE platform is its 
scalability to wafer-level manufacturing. Since femtosecond laser writing is a software-controlled, 
maskless process, multiple LWVC devices can be made sequentially or in parallel on a single, 
large fused-silica wafer. All subsequent processing steps (HF etching, getter insertion, bonding, 
and laser activation) are also compatible with wafer-scale batch processing, as is well-established 
for FLICE technology \cite{corrielli2021}. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:lwvc_wafer}, this approach 
enables the simultaneous fabrication of tens to hundreds of identical devices per substrate, 
dramatically reducing per-unit cost and opening a clear path toward mass production of chip-scale 
quantum sensors.

\subsection{State of the Art and Performance}
\label{sec:lwvc_state}
The state of the art shows that this technology is maturing rapidly. 
Originally designed with millimeter-scale sensing channels (\SI{1}{\milli\meter} 
$\times$ \SI{1}{\milli\meter} cross-section), 
LWVCs have now successfully scaled down to the micro-scale. Recent devices 
feature sub-millimeter sensing channels with a tightly confined cross-section (\SI{500}{\micro\meter} 
$\times$ \SI{500}{\micro\meter}), optimized for optically pumped magnetometry through the addition of 
\SI{0.75}{amg} of nitrogen buffer gas to suppress wall-collision relaxation and eliminate radiation 
trapping. Operating in the zero-field resonance (ZFR) regime (Section~\ref{sec:opm}) 
within a detection volume of only \SI{2.25}{\milli\meter\cubed}, these devices achieve a 
magnetic sensitivity of approximately \SI{1}{\pico\tesla\per\sqrt{\hertz}} at \SI{10}{\hertz} \cite{zanoni2024}. 
This performance puts the LWVCs at the forefront of compact, high-resolution 
magnetometry, making them competitive with much larger vapor cells and close to the performance of 
bulk optically pumped magnetometers.

Looking forward, atomic-shot-noise calculations for the current sensing volume project a fundamental 
sensitivity limit of $\approx$ \SI{18}{\femto\tesla\per\sqrt{\hertz}} at the optimal buffer gas density of \SI{7.1}{amg} 
\cite{zanoni2024}, showing that the current \SI{1}{\pico\tesla\per\sqrt{\hertz}} performance is limited by technical 
noise rather than fundamental atomic physics, meaning that substantial improvements are still possible as 
the technology matures. Furthermore, the operating temperature of the current devices 
(\SIrange{90}{100}{\celsius}) is compatible 
with standard thermal isolation strategies for on-chip deployment \cite{zanoni2024}.

Beyond precision magnetometry (explored in Section~\ref{sec:applications}), 
this architecture opens new sensing applications, including microfluidic zero-to-ultralow-field NMR, 
portable biomagnetic diagnostics, detection of magnetic nanoparticles and magnetotactic bacteria, 
and Rydberg-atom electrometry.
