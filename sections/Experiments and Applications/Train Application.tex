\section{Corrosion Inspection for Railway Tracks}
\label{sec:opm}
\label{sec:applications}
A new generation of highly sensitive and precise magnetic sensors based on 
vapor cells has recently transitioned from development to the commercial 
stage. These devices, known as \textit{Optically Pumped Magnetometers} (OPMs), 
can detect extraordinarily weak magnetic fields, with sensitivities reaching 
the pico-tesla ($\mathrm{pT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$) or even femto-tesla 
($\mathrm{fT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$) scale. Crucially for practical applications, 
they maintain this extreme precision while operating within a wide dynamic range, 
allowing them to function reliably even in unshielded environments \cite{quspin, deans2021}.

The core operating principle of these sensors relies on using laser light to 
manipulate and measure the quantum spin states of an alkali metal gas 
(typically $^{87}\mathrm{Rb}$ or $^{133}\mathrm{Cs}$) enclosed in a glass 
cell \cite{tierney2019}. This physical process occurs in three consecutive 
stages, beginning with the preparation of the atomic state through optical 
pumping. During this first stage, a circularly polarized ($\sigma^+$) laser 
beam, called the pump beam, passes through the vapor and transfers its angular 
momentum to the alkali atoms, thereby aligning their spins in a specific 
direction. Concurrently, a constant magnetic field ($B_{\mathrm{DC}}$) is 
applied parallel to this laser to guide and stabilize the aligned atoms into a 
baseline energy state or Zeeman sublevel.

Once the atoms are prepared, the second stage involves their resonant 
excitation by means of an alternating radio-frequency magnetic field 
($B_{\mathrm{RF}}$) applied perpendicularly to the baseline field. When 
the frequency of this radio-frequency field matches the natural precession 
frequency of the atoms—known as the Larmor frequency ($\omega_L = \gamma B_{\mathrm{tot}}$, 
where $\gamma$ is the gyromagnetic ratio)—it drives a coherent transition in 
the atomic spins, forcing them to rotate synchronously in circles. 

Finally, the third stage consists of the optical interrogation and electronic 
demodulation of the system. To detect the atomic rotation without destroying 
the prepared quantum state, a second, linearly polarized laser beam, designated 
as the probe beam, is sent through the cell. As this probe light passes through 
the spinning atoms, its plane of polarization undergoes a periodic rotation due 
to the paramagnetic Faraday effect, oscillating at the exact frequency of the 
Larmor precession. A balanced polarimeter captures this oscillating light and 
converts it into an alternating electrical signal (AC). This signal is subsequently 
processed by a lock-in amplifier, which demodulates the AC signal using the 
radio-frequency field as a reference, yielding precise direct current (DC) outputs 
for both amplitude and phase. With this configuration, any shifts in the local magnetic 
field disrupt the atomic resonance, producing an immediate, proportional change 
in these DC voltage outputs.

orthogonal arrangement of the laser beams and the magnetic fields, is illustrated

Several non-destructive inspection (NDI) experiments have successfully demonstrated 
the use of atomic magnetometers for Electromagnetic Induction Imaging (EMI) 
\cite{marmugi2020} and Magnetic Induction Tomography (MIT) \cite{wickenbrock2014}. 
Recent studies \cite{guilizzoni2016, deans2024} have characterized 
the penetration capabilities of these devices through structural barriers. 
It has been established that OPMs can achieve 
high-fidelity object imaging through thick conductive enclosures under unshielded, 
room-temperature conditions without requiring background subtraction, as shown 
in Figure~\ref{fig:opm_images}. Moreover, fully portable scanning heads equipped 
with active-compensation coils have been demonstrated, proving capable of operating 
within complex, real-world magnetic environments \cite{deans2021}.

When microfabricated into advanced architectures such as LWVCs, OPMs can 
achieve ultra-compact footprints at the MEMS scale with minimal power 
requirements. By eliminating the bulky cryogenic cooling systems inherent 
to conventional Superconducting Quantum Interference Devices (SQUIDs), these 
miniaturized atomic sensors enable highly portable operations and scalable, 
multi-channel array configurations.

A standard railway track profile (Figure~\ref{fig:rail_profile}) 
is analyzed. These tracks are manufactured from high-strength 
pearlitic carbon steel, with high electrical conductivity 
($\sigma_{\mathrm{rail}} \approx 4.0$--$5.0 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{S/m}$) 
and strong ferromagnetic permeability 
($\mu_{r,\mathrm{rail}} \approx 50$--$200$). 
When exposed to atmospheric oxygen and moisture, electrochemical 
oxidation produces a degradation layer of iron oxides and 
hydroxides (primarily $\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}$, $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$, 
and $\mathrm{FeOOH}$) at the rail foot. This corrosion layer 
presents a dramatically lower electrical conductivity 
($\sigma_{\mathrm{corr}} \approx 10^{-2}$--$10^2\,\mathrm{S/m}$) 
and a relative permeability that drops to near unity 
($\mu_{r,\mathrm{corr}} \approx 1$--$5$), rendering it 
effectively paramagnetic.

The detection principle is as follows. An excitation coil 
driven by an alternating current generates a time-varying 
primary magnetic field $B_1(t) = B_1 e^{i\omega t}$, which 
penetrates the rail and induces eddy currents 
($\mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E}$) in the bulk steel according 
to Faraday's law. The amplitude of these currents decays 
exponentially with depth, characterized by the skin depth:

\begin{equation}
    \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}} 
           = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\pi f \mu_0 \mu_r \sigma}},
    \label{eq:skin_depth}
\end{equation}

where $f$ is the excitation frequency, $\mu_0$ is the vacuum 
permeability, $\mu_r$ is the relative permeability, and 
$\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity of the domain. 
Because the corrosion layer presents a severe contrast in 
both $\mu_r$ and $\sigma$ relative to healthy steel, the 
eddy current distribution is perturbed in the vicinity of 
the corroded region, altering the secondary field $B_2$ that 
it generates. The total field $B_{\mathrm{tot}} = B_1 + B_2$ 
thus carries a signature of the corrosion that can be resolved 
by the OPM.

\subsection{Finite-Element Simulation}

To evaluate the feasibility of this concept prior to 
experimental implementation, a comprehensive 2D 
finite-element method (\gls{FEM}) simulation was carried out in 
COMSOL Multiphysics, using the \textit{AC/DC Module} 
(\textit{Magnetic Fields} physics interface) in the 
frequency-domain formulation. Two scenarios are modelled: 
a healthy rail (benchmark) and a defective rail containing 
a localized corrosion layer of variable depth at the foot 
(Figure~\ref{fig:clearance_line}).

\paragraph{Geometry and boundary conditions.}
The simulation domain consists of the 2D cross-section of a 
standard rail profile, surrounded by a sufficiently large air 
region ($\sim$10 rail widths on each side) to avoid artefacts 
from the outer boundary. The outer boundary of the air domain 
is assigned a magnetic insulation condition 
($\mathbf{n} \times \mathbf{A} = 0$, where $\mathbf{A}$ is 
the magnetic vector potential), which is appropriate for a 
magnetically open domain at distances far from the source. 
The excitation coil is modelled as a single rectangular 
conductor carrying a prescribed current density 
$\mathbf{J}_e$ (ampere-turns per unit area).

\paragraph{Mesh strategy.}
Accurate resolution of the electromagnetic fields requires the 
mesh to respect the skin depth constraint at each frequency. 
In the steel and corrosion domains, the maximum element size 
is set to $\delta/3$ to ensure at least three elements per 
skin depth, following standard FEM practice for eddy-current 
problems \cite{comsol_ref}. Since $\delta$ varies from 
$\sim$7~mm at $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$ to $\sim$0.07~mm at 
$100\,\mathrm{kHz}$ for the rail steel, frequency-dependent 
adaptive meshes are generated for each simulation point in 
the parameter sweep. In the air region, where no skin effect 
applies, a free triangular mesh with maximum element size 
constrained to one tenth of the smallest geometric feature 
is used. The corrosion layer is meshed with a boundary layer 
refinement of five inflation layers at its interfaces with 
both the steel and the air gap. The resulting meshes 
contain between $8 \times 10^4$ and $3 \times 10^5$ elements 
depending on frequency, with the highest element counts at 
high frequencies where $\delta$ is smallest.

\paragraph{Material assignments and parametric sweep.}
The rail steel is assigned 
$\sigma_{\mathrm{rail}} = 4.5 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{S/m}$ 
and $\mu_{r,\mathrm{rail}} = 100$ (representative values 
for pearlitic rail steel). The corrosion layer is assigned 
$\sigma_{\mathrm{corr}} = 1\,\mathrm{S/m}$ and 
$\mu_{r,\mathrm{corr}} = 2$. The simulation sweeps 
excitation frequencies from $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$ to 
$100\,\mathrm{kHz}$ over a logarithmic grid of 30 points 
per decade, and corrosion layer depths from 0 (healthy) to 
$5\,\mathrm{mm}$ in $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ steps, under three 
representative coil current strengths.

\paragraph{Evaluation cutpoints and operational constraints.}
The simulation imposes two simultaneous operational 
constraints on any candidate sensor position:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic range boundary:} The total field 
    $B_{\mathrm{tot}}$ must not exceed the QuSpin 
    linear operating range of $150{,}000\,\mathrm{nT}$ 
    to prevent sensor saturation.
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity boundary:} The differential 
    field between healthy and corroded states must satisfy 
    $\Delta B = |B_{\mathrm{healthy}} - B_{\mathrm{corroded}}| 
    \geq 3\,\mathrm{pT}$, the scalar noise floor of the sensor.
\end{enumerate}
Three cutpoints above the mechanical clearance line are 
evaluated: $P_1(x_1, y_1)$, $P_2(x_2, y_2)$, and 
$P_3(x_3, y_3)$, at increasing heights above the rail 
surface.

The results reveal a characteristic trade-off governed by 
the frequency-dependent skin depth. At low frequencies 
($f < 100\,\mathrm{Hz}$), the field penetrates the full 
depth of the rail foot, but the slow rate of flux change 
produces weak eddy currents and a differential response 
$\Delta B$ near or below the sensor noise floor. At high 
frequencies ($f > 50\,\mathrm{kHz}$), the skin effect 
confines the field to a thin surface layer 
($\delta \to 0$), preventing interaction with the interior 
of the corrosion zone.

An optimal operating window is identified in the 
intermediate band of $1$--$20\,\mathrm{kHz}$, where the 
skin depth matches the spatial scale of the expected 
corrosion layers. Within this band, $\Delta B$ peaks 
sharply and, at cutpoints $P_1$ and $P_2$, successfully 
exceeds the $3\,\mathrm{pT}$ threshold while the 
background field remains below the $150{,}000\,\mathrm{nT}$ 
dynamic range limit of the QuSpin sensor. These findings 
validate the technical feasibility of using unshielded, 
compact LWVC-based OPMs for non-contact, real-time railway 
track corrosion monitoring, providing a clear path toward 
automated structural health evaluation systems.
