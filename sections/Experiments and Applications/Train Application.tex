\section{Corrosion Inspection for Railway Tracks}
\label{sec:opm}
\label{sec:applications}
A new generation of highly sensitive and precise magnetic sensors based on 
vapor cells has recently transitioned from development to the commercial 
stage. These devices, known as \textit{Optically Pumped Magnetometers} (OPMs), 
can detect extraordinarily weak magnetic fields, with sensitivities reaching 
the pico-tesla ($\mathrm{pT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$) or even femto-tesla 
($\mathrm{fT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$) scale. Crucially for practical applications, 
they maintain this extreme precision while operating within a wide dynamic range, 
allowing them to function reliably even in unshielded environments \cite{quspin, deans2021}.

The core operating principle of these sensors relies on using laser light to 
manipulate and measure the quantum spin states of an alkali metal gas 
(typically $^{87}\mathrm{Rb}$ or $^{133}\mathrm{Cs}$) enclosed in a glass 
cell \cite{tierney2019}. This physical process occurs in three consecutive 
stages, beginning with the preparation of the atomic state through optical 
pumping. During this first stage, a circularly polarized ($\sigma^+$) laser 
beam, called the pump beam, passes through the vapor and transfers its angular 
momentum to the alkali atoms, thereby aligning their spins in a specific 
direction. Concurrently, a constant magnetic field ($B_{\mathrm{DC}}$) is 
applied parallel to this laser to guide and stabilize the aligned atoms into a 
baseline energy state or Zeeman sublevel.

Once the atoms are prepared, the second stage involves their resonant 
excitation by means of an alternating radio-frequency magnetic field 
($B_{\mathrm{RF}}$) applied perpendicularly to the baseline field. When 
the frequency of this radio-frequency field matches the natural precession 
frequency of the atoms—known as the Larmor frequency ($\omega_L = \gamma B_{\mathrm{tot}}$, 
where $\gamma$ is the gyromagnetic ratio)—it drives a coherent transition in 
the atomic spins, forcing them to rotate synchronously in circles. 

Finally, the third stage consists of the optical interrogation and electronic 
demodulation of the system. To detect the atomic rotation without destroying 
the prepared quantum state, a second, linearly polarized laser beam, designated 
as the probe beam, is sent through the cell. As this probe light passes through 
the spinning atoms, its plane of polarization undergoes a periodic rotation due 
to the paramagnetic Faraday effect, oscillating at the exact frequency of the 
Larmor precession. A balanced polarimeter captures this oscillating light and 
converts it into an alternating electrical signal (AC). This signal is subsequently 
processed by a lock-in amplifier, which demodulates the AC signal using the 
radio-frequency field as a reference, yielding precise direct current (DC) outputs 
for both amplitude and phase. With this configuration, any shifts in the local magnetic 
field disrupt the atomic resonance, producing an immediate, proportional change 
in these DC voltage outputs.

The schematic layout of this radio-frequency OPM configuration, including the 
orthogonal arrangement of the laser beams and the magnetic fields, is illustrated 
in Figure~\ref{fig:opm_setup}.

Several non-destructive inspection (NDI) experiments have successfully demonstrated 
the use of atomic magnetometers for Electromagnetic Induction Imaging (EMI) 
\cite{marmugi2020} and Magnetic Induction Tomography (MIT) \cite{wickenbrock2014}. 
Recent studies \cite{guilizzoni2016, deans2024} have characterized 
the penetration capabilities of these devices through structural barriers. 
It has been established that OPMs can achieve 
high-fidelity object imaging through thick conductive enclosures under unshielded, 
room-temperature conditions without requiring background subtraction, as shown 
in Figure~\ref{fig:opm_images}. Moreover, fully portable scanning heads equipped 
with active-compensation coils have been demonstrated, proving capable of operating 
within complex, real-world magnetic environments \cite{deans2021}.

When microfabricated into advanced architectures such as LWVCs, OPMs can 
achieve ultra-compact footprints at the MEMS scale with minimal power 
requirements. By eliminating the bulky cryogenic cooling systems inherent 
to conventional Superconducting Quantum Interference Devices (SQUIDs), these 
miniaturized atomic sensors enable highly portable operations and scalable, 
multi-channel array configurations.

\subsection{Application Scenario: Railway Track Corrosion}
\label{sec:corrosion_scenario}

Building upon these non-destructive capabilities, this section introduces a
novel industrial application designed to leverage the extreme precision of
atomic magnetometry. The primary objective is to deploy an OPM integrated
with an LWVC sensor head to detect the onset and volumetric progression of
structural corrosion at the foot of operational railway tracks. To establish
a realistic baseline, the present work references a commercial QuSpin
Total-Field Magnetometer as the benchmark sensor configuration. This device
operates as a pulsed laser-pumped rubidium free-induction-decay
magnetometer, with its core technical characteristics outlined in
Table~\ref{tab:quspin_specs}. Of paramount importance to this real-world
application are its scalar sensitivity of $<3\,\mathrm{pT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$
and a dynamic range spanning from $1$ to $150\,\mathrm{\mu T}$, which
effectively covers the ambient magnetic environments encountered in
unshielded railway settings. Furthermore, the compact physical footprint of
the sensor head ($12\,\mathrm{g}$, $17.7 \times 19.8 \times 35.8\,%
\mathrm{mm}^3$) makes it highly compatible with portable, automated
scanning assemblies \cite{quspin}.

The tracking infrastructure under analysis adheres to a standard structural
rail profile.
These tracks are typically manufactured from high-strength pearlitic carbon
steel, characterized by a high electrical conductivity ($\sigma_{\mathrm{rail}}
\approx 10^6\,\mathrm{S/m}$) and a robust ferromagnetic relative
permeability ($\mu_{r,\mathrm{rail}} \approx 10^2$). When exposed to
atmospheric oxygen and persistent moisture, electrochemical oxidation
generates a localized degradation layer composed of iron oxides and
hydroxides—primarily $\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}$, $\mathrm{Fe_3O_4}$, and
$\mathrm{FeOOH}$—predominantly at the vulnerable rail foot region. This
resulting corrosion layer exhibits a severe material contrast compared to
the healthy steel substrate, as its electrical conductivity drops
dramatically ($\sigma_{\mathrm{corr}} \approx 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{S/m}$) and
its relative permeability falls to near unity ($\mu_{r,\mathrm{corr}}
\approx 1$), effectively rendering the domain paramagnetic.

The underlying detection mechanism exploits these electromagnetic contrasts
via eddy current induction. An excitation coil driven by an alternating
current produces a time-varying primary magnetic field, $B_1(t) = B_1
e^{i\omega t}$, which penetrates the conductive rail volume and induces
localized eddy currents ($\mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E}$) according to
Faraday's law. The amplitude of these induced currents decays exponentially
with depth as a function of the electromagnetic skin depth ($\delta$),
defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}
           = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\pi f \mu_0 \mu_r \sigma}}
    \label{eq:skin_depth}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $f$ represents the operational excitation frequency, $\mu_0$
is the vacuum permeability, $\mu_r$ is the relative permeability, and
$\sigma$ denotes the electrical conductivity of the respective domain.
Because the structural corrosion layer introduces a severe discontinuity in
both permeability and conductivity, the local eddy current distribution
becomes significantly perturbed. This disruption alters the secondary
magnetic field ($B_2$) generated by the currents, meaning the total magnetic
field ($B_{\mathrm{tot}} = B_1 + B_2$) carries a distinct electromagnetic
signature of the subsurface defect that can be resolved by the high-precision
OPM sensor.

\subsection{Finite-Element Simulation}
\label{sec:fem_simulation}

To evaluate the mathematical feasibility and physical constraints of this
diagnostic concept prior to full experimental assembly, a comprehensive 2D
finite-element method (\gls{FEM}) simulation was executed within COMSOL
Multiphysics. Developed as a preliminary proof of concept, this numerical
framework forms part of an ongoing project at QSENSATO Srl. and reflects
a diagnostic system that is currently in its development phase. Operating via
the \textit{AC/DC Module}\footnote{It is, indeed, a long way to the top if you
wanna rock and roll.} and utilizing the \textit{Magnetic Fields} physics
interface in a frequency-domain formulation, the framework compares a
baseline healthy rail against a defective track profile containing a
localized corrosion zone of variable depth at the rail foot.

\paragraph{Geometry, boundary conditions, and material properties.}
As depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:clearance_line}, the geometric domain
comprises the 2D cross-section of a standard rail profile (a) secured by
native fastening clamps (b). A localized corrosion domain (c) is embedded
directly at the structural foot of the rail. The AC excitation coils are
positioned a few centimeters above the upper rail surface (d) and are
modeled as two distinct rectangular conductor domains carrying a prescribed
external current density $\mathbf{J}_e$. The entire engineering assembly
is enclosed within a spacious air region (e) to eliminate unphysical
boundary constraints. The outermost boundaries of this air domain are
governed by a magnetic insulation condition ($\mathbf{n} \times \mathbf{A}
= 0$, where $\mathbf{A}$ represents the magnetic vector potential),
simulating an open domain at an infinite distance from the sources. Beneath
the track assembly, a structural granite foundation layer (f) is
implemented to simulate the electromagnetic feedback of real ballast stones.
Finally, to allow the field solutions to attenuate naturally without
artificial reflections, an Infinite Element Domain layer (g) encapsulates
the outermost perimeter of the setup. Within this model, the OPM is
represented by a spatial evaluation \textit{cutpoint}, subject to the
strict operational constraint that it must remain positioned above the
mechanical clearance line of the rail.

In terms of material property assignments, the bulk rail steel is designated
with an electrical conductivity of $\sigma_{\mathrm{rail}} = 6 \times
10^6\,\mathrm{S/m}$ and a relative magnetic permeability of
$\mu_{r,\mathrm{rail}} = 400$, which are highly representative of pearlitic
railway tracks. The corrosion defect (assumed to consist primarily of
$\mathrm{Fe_2O_3}$) is assigned a degraded conductivity of
$\sigma_{\mathrm{corr}} = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{S/m}$ and a relative permeability
of $\mu_{r,\mathrm{corr}} = 5$. The surrounding air and infinite element
domains are treated as standard non-conductive, non-magnetic environments
($\sigma = 0\,\mathrm{S/m}$, $\mu_r = 1$), while the underlying granite
ballast is modeled as a lossy dielectric foundation with a conductivity of
$\sigma_{\mathrm{granite}} = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{S/m}$ and a non-magnetic relative
permeability of $\mu_{r,\mathrm{granite}} = 1$.

\paragraph{Mesh strategy.}
An accurate evaluation of the secondary fields requires a specialized,
frequency-dependent dynamic meshing strategy capable of adjusting to the
changing skin depth ($\delta$) of the system. Because $\delta$ scales
inversely with the square root of the coil's excitation frequency, the
spatial resolution of the mesh must adapt dynamically to avoid severe
discretization errors. To properly capture the rapid exponential decay of
the fields and the subtle perturbations induced by the corrosion, a
significantly smaller, refined mesh is enforced near the regions of
interest, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_composite}.

This multi-scale discretization strategy comprises a general overview of the
entire computational domain (Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_composite}a), a dedicated
refinement at the upper section enclosing the excitation coils and the rail
head (Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_composite}b), and a localized refinement at the
bottom part containing the fastening clamps and the corrosion domain
(Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_composite}c). Most notably, a dense boundary layer
meshing is applied directly at the corrosion interface
(Figure~\ref{fig:mesh_composite}d) to ensure at least three elements per
skin depth layer. Given that $\delta$ shifts from approximately
\SI{7}{\milli\meter} at a low frequency of $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$ down to a
microscopic scale of \SI{0.07}{\milli\meter} at $100\,\mathrm{kHz}$ for
bulk steel, this adaptive parametric mesh is generated automatically for each
discrete frequency point. Conversely, within the air and ballast domains
where skin effect phenomena are absent, a free triangular mesh with loose
constraints between \SI{0.1}{\centi\meter} and \SI{1}{\centi\meter} is
utilized. The fine corrosion layer is further resolved using a boundary
layer refinement technique consisting of five structured inflation layers
along its structural interfaces, resulting in total mesh counts that vary
from thousands to tens of thousands of elements depending on the excitation
frequency.

\paragraph{Parametric sweep and engineering optimization.}
The simulation sweeps excitation frequencies from $1\,\mathrm{Hz}$ to
$2\,\mathrm{kHz}$, and square corrosion with size from 0 (healthy) to
$2.5\,\mathrm{mm} \times 2.5\,\mathrm{mm}$, under three representative coil
current strengths. The primary engineering objective of this parametric study
is to navigate a critical trade-off between sensor saturation and detection
sensitivity. While the OPM must be positioned near the track to resolve the
weak magnetic anomalies, the primary field generated by the excitation coils
is intensely strong and can easily overwhelm the sensor. To mitigate this
risk and prevent sensor blinding, the OPM must be positioned at an optimized
stand-off distance further away from the rail surface. Simultaneously, the
excitation frequency of the coil must be carefully optimized; higher
frequencies enhance the electromagnetic contrast within the shallow corrosion
layer but drastically reduce field penetration into the bulk steel.
Consequently, the simulation sweeps aim to identify a highly specific
operating window where the coil frequency and the sensor height are perfectly
balanced.

\paragraph{Evaluation cutpoints and operational constraints.}
The simulation imposes two simultaneous operational constraints on any
candidate sensor position above the mechanical clearance line:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic range boundary:} The total field $B_{\mathrm{tot}}$
    must not exceed the QuSpin linear operating range of $150{,}000\,\mathrm{nT}$
    to prevent sensor saturation.
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity boundary:} The differential field between
    healthy and corroded states must satisfy $\Delta B =
    |B_{\mathrm{healthy}} - B_{\mathrm{corroded}}| \geq 3\,\mathrm{pT}$, the
    scalar noise floor of the sensor.
\end{enumerate}
Three cutpoints above the mechanical clearance line are evaluated:
$P_1(x_1, y_1)$, $P_2(x_2, y_2)$, and $P_3(x_3, y_3)$, at increasing heights
above the rail surface.
