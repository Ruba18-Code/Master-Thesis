\section{Experimental Investigation of Optical Contact Bonding}
\label{sec:experimental_optical_contact_bonding}
\textbf{TABLE UNFINISHED}
Section~\ref{sec:optical_contact_bonding} established the theoretical foundations, 
governing criteria, and idealized surface quality requirements of optical contact bonding, this 
section shifts focus to its
practical implementation. To evaluate the feasibility and reliability of
the sealing process under non-ideal conditions, a series of optical
contact bonding experiments were conducted at the Chemistry Department of
the University of Bari (UniBa), Italy. The samples utilized for these tests
were fused silica disks with a diameter of \SI{30}{\milli\meter},
characterized by a total thickness variation (TTV) of less than
\SI{0.5}{\micro\meter} and an average surface roughness ($R_a$) of less
than \SI{0.5}{\nano\meter}. A comprehensive reference log is provided in the
Appendix (Table~\ref{tab:annex_bonding_log}), which includes both a thorough
compilation of bonding recipes established in the literature as well as a
detailed summary of the specific experimental procedures executed.

\subsection{Cleaning Protocols and Surface Activation}
To achieve successful optical contact, the surfaces must be exceptionally
clean and highly hydrophilic. It is critical to emphasize that these
experiments were conducted outside of a dedicated cleanroom environment.
The absence of a strictly controlled, particle-free atmosphere introduces
a significant degree of environmental randomness and variability to the
trials. Because the bonding mechanism relies on short-range intermolecular
forces, even microscopic airborne dust particles or contaminants can become
trapped between the substrates, creating localized voids and completely
preventing the spontaneous propagation of the bonding wave.

Several cleaning and activation protocols were evaluated to mitigate this
environmental limitation and determine the most robust preparation strategy.
\paragraph{Ethanol and DIW Procedure.}
The initial trial consisted of a simple, rapid soaking sequence using
ethanol followed by a deionized water (DIW) rinse (\ce{Ethanol} + \ce{DIW}).
The samples were subsequently dried by vigorously shaking them for a few
seconds. Out of the pairs prepared with this rudimentary method, two
successfully bonded. Following this initial test, all subsequent procedures
were strictly performed under a chemical hood utilizing an ultrasonic cleaner
to ensure proper contamination removal (Figure~\ref{fig:hood_setup}).
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Images/chemical_hood.jpeg}
    \caption{Experimental setup at UniBa, showing the chemical hood and the
    ultrasonic cleaner utilized for the controlled cleaning protocols.}
    \label{fig:hood_setup}
\end{figure}
A physical activation protocol relied exclusively on oxygen plasma. The
disks were subjected to a quick initial pre-wash using acetone followed
by a deionized water rinse (\ce{Acetone} + \ce{DIW}) before being
immediately transferred into an \ce{O2} plasma system (700~\text{mTorr},
\SI{30}{\watt} RF power), as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:plasma_treatment}. 
Preliminary trials utilizing shorter plasma exposure durations, starting from 
1 minute, were systematically executed but yielded no successful or spontaneous 
adhesion. Consequently, 5 minutes was identified as the minimum operational 
threshold required for sufficient surface activation under the specific 
pressure, power, and gas dynamics of the utilized system. To evaluate the 
influence of exposure duration beyond this hardware threshold on the bonding 
kinematics, a parametric optimization sweep was established with configurations 
at 5, 6, 8, and 10 minutes. A single sample pair was processed for each of the 
5-minute, 6-minute, and 8-minute exposure windows, while two independent pairs 
were treated using the 10-minute exposure time.

    \caption{Oxygen (\ce{O2}) plasma treatment of the fused silica disks.
    The activated plasma gas imparts a characteristic purple glow to the

    \label{fig:plasma_treatment}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{DIW, Piranha, and DIW Procedure.}
A wet-chemical activation approach was tested using a heated acid bath
(\ce{DIW} + \ce{Piranha} + \ce{DIW}). The disks were first soaked in DIW for
2 minutes, followed by a 20-minute immersion in a piranha solution
(\ce{H2SO4}:\ce{H2O2} in a 3:1 ratio) heated to \SI{70}{\celsius} to remove
organic residues and hydroxylate the silica surfaces. After the piranha bath,
the disks were soaked in DIW for 5 minutes to eliminate any acidic residues
and were extensively dried using a high-pressure \ce{N2} gun
(Figure~\ref{fig:n2_gun}). Three independent samples were prepared using this
protocol to carefully verify reproducibility outside a cleanroom.
\paragraph{Sequential DIW, RCA-1, DIW, Piranha, and DIW Procedure.}
A multi-step chemical protocol was evaluated to leverage both particle
removal and strong oxidation (\ce{DIW} + \ce{RCA-1} + \ce{DIW} + \ce{Piranha}
+ \ce{DIW}). The disks were rinsed with DIW and immersed in an RCA-1 solution
for \SI{10}{\minute} at \SI{70}{\celsius}. Following another DIW rinse, the
samples were treated with a piranha solution for \SI{10}{\minute} at
\SI{70}{\celsius} to further hydroxylate the surface. Finally, after a
thorough 5-minute DIW rinse to remove acid traces, the disks were dried using
the high-pressure \ce{N2} gun. Three samples were successfully processed.
\paragraph{Sequential DIW, Piranha, DIW, and Plasma Procedure.}
The final protocol integrated both chemical and physical activation methods
(\ce{DIW} + \ce{Piranha} + \ce{DIW} + \ce{Plasma}). The disks underwent an
initial DIW rinse, followed by the 20-minute piranha treatment and subsequent
DIW rinse, after which the high-pressure \ce{N2} drying step was applied. This
was immediately followed by a 10-minute \ce{O2} plasma activation
(700~\text{mTorr}, \SI{30}{\watt}). A total of 2 individual samples were
processed using this configuration.

\subsection{Contacting and Curing}
Regardless of the cleaning protocol applied, the physical contacting step was
identical for all samples. The treated disks were brought into contact using
a custom-designed alignment fixture (Figure~\ref{fig:holding_piece}) and were
subsequently pressed firmly by hand for approximately one minute to initiate
the spontaneous bonding wave.

In standard optical contact bonding, a high-temperature annealing step is
usually performed to convert the weak hydrogen bonds into strong, permanent
covalent siloxane bridges. However, whenever the bonded disks were placed in
the available oven for annealing, complete debonding was observed after a few
hours. This failure was attributed to an excessively steep temperature ramp
rate. If the temperature rises too rapidly, the interfacial water trapped
between the disks evaporates and turns into steam faster than it can diffuse
towards the outer edges. This generates an internal pressure that
forces the disks apart. Lacking access to an oven capable of highly
controlled, slow thermal ramping, the high-temperature annealing step was
discarded. Instead, the contacted pairs were left to cure at room temperature
for several days.

non-cleanroom environment, the bonded samples were optically inspected and
analyzed using the Fiji (ImageJ) software suite. By processing the raw optical
photographs into high-contrast binary maps, the precise percentage of the
successfully bonded area versus the void area was calculated.

\subsection{Tensile Testing}
To evaluate the mechanical robustness of the optical contact bonds, a series
of tensile tests were performed on the successfully bonded pairs. Evaluating
the tensile strength is critical to ensure that the sealed vapor cells can
withstand internal pressure variations and mechanical shocks during operation,
as previously explained in Section~\ref{sec:bonding_strength_tensile}.

To prepare the experimental setup, the bonded fused silica disks were first
rigidly attached to custom-designed 3D-printed plastic fixtures using a
high-strength adhesive layer (see Figure~\ref{fig:3d_fixture}). 
This structural adhesive integration ensures a
uniform transfer of the tensile load across the entire area of the substrates
without causing premature edge de-adhesion. This setup is illustrated in 
Figure~\ref{fig:tensile_setup}

The tensile loading process continued until mechanical failure occurred, at
which point the optical contact bond fractured and the disks separated. The
dynamometer automatically captured and registered the peak normal force at the
exact moment of debonding. This ultimate tensile force was subsequently
converted into its corresponding maximum supported pressure values by dividing
the recorded peak load by the effective bonded area calculated from the prior
optical analysis.

Table~\ref{tab:tensile_results} summarizes the mechanical performance of the
bonded pairs across the different activation protocols. The effective
bonded area percentages, extracted from the prior Fiji (ImageJ) optical
analysis, are directly correlated with the maximum supported pressure
measured during the tensile tests.
